Pick up to 7 countries and look at their house prices in five ways. "After inflation" shows real buying power, and "In US dollars" shows what a US investor would have made, currency moves included. The table under the chart gives the numbers for the same period.

The numbers

Yearly average is the compound growth rate over the period. Best and worst 12 months are the biggest rise and fall on a year earlier within the period. Biggest drop is the largest fall from a high inside the period. Swings is how much prices move around, as a yearly standard deviation of quarterly changes. Inflation and currency show how much of the price change came from them: a rise in prices with high inflation can still mean a loss in real terms, and a weak currency can wipe out gains for a US investor.

How this works

  • House prices: the BIS residential property price index for each country, as published by its central bank or statistics office. Coverage differs by country (some are whole-country, some big cities, some new homes only), so compare changes, not levels.
  • After inflation: the BIS real index, the price index divided by consumer prices.
  • In US dollars: the nominal index divided by that quarter's average exchange rate against the US dollar (BIS). It is what a US-based investor gained or lost, before rent, costs and taxes.
  • Interest rate: the central bank's policy rate (euro area countries use the ECB rate). Falling rates usually make mortgages cheaper and support prices.
  • Value: the OECD price-to-income ratio against the country's own long-run average (100 = average, below 100 = cheaper than usual) where it exists, otherwise how far real prices sit below their all-time high.
  • Investor score (0 to 100): where a country ranks against the others on five things: real price momentum (30%), 10-year real growth (20%), value (25%), the change in interest rates over the year (15%) and the currency against the US dollar (10%). A missing part is left out and the rest reweighted. Countries under international sanctions (Russia) are shown but not scored. It is a starting point for research, not a forecast.

These are quarterly national statistics, not live prices, and they don't include rents, buying costs, taxes or rules on foreign buyers, which vary a lot between countries. Not financial advice.
